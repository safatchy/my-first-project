## Understanding Your Seafood

Before you heat a single pan or slice a single chilli, it helps to truly understand what you're working with. Chilli squid and scallops may both come from the sea, but they are vastly different creatures — in texture, in temperament, and in what they ask of the cook. Knowing them well is the first step toward cooking them brilliantly.

### Chilli Squid — What It Is & Why It's Beloved in England

Walk into any Chinese takeaway in England on a Friday night and you'll find it on the menu: salt and chilli squid. Order a sharing starter at Wagamama, and there it is again — crispy, dusted in shichimi, served with that tangy vinegar dip that's become iconic. Browse the specials board at a seaside gastropub in Devon or Cornwall, and you'll see it once more, perhaps under the name "crispy calamari with chilli and lime."

But what exactly is chilli squid — and how does it differ from calamari?

The distinction is largely cultural. **Calamari** is the Italian term for squid, typically referring to rings or strips that are lightly battered and deep-fried, then served simply with lemon and aioli. It's the Mediterranean approach — understated, golden, letting the seafood speak.

**Chilli squid** — sometimes called salt and pepper squid or salt and chilli squid — is the East and Southeast Asian interpretation. The squid is coated in a lighter, starch-based dusting (cornflour or potato starch rather than a wet batter), fried until shatteringly crisp, and then either tossed with chillies, garlic, salt, and spring onions, or served alongside a chilli-spiked dipping sauce. It's bolder. It has attitude. And it has become, arguably, one of the most ordered starters in England over the past two decades.

The reason is simple: it delivers on every level. Crunch. Heat. Sweetness from the squid itself. A hit of salt. The satisfaction of something fried done perfectly. It's pub food and fine dining all at once — democratic, unpretentious, and utterly addictive.

British squid is readily available at fishmongers across England — it's caught in waters around the UK coast and is often fresher and more affordable than people expect.

### Scallops — The King of British Shellfish

If chilli squid is the loud, confident friend who lights up the room, scallops are the one who says very little but leaves the deepest impression.


In the UK, we have access to three main types:

- **King scallops** (*Pecten maximus*) — The large, luxurious ones you see on restaurant menus. Sweet, meaty, and roughly 10–15cm across. These are the stars of a seared scallop dish.
- **Queen scallops** (queenies) — Smaller, sweeter, and traditionally harvested in abundance around the Isle of Man, Scotland, and Lyme Bay. Wonderful in pasta, risotto, or eaten simply with vinegar as a childhood snack (as chef Mark Hix fondly remembers from his Dorset upbringing).
- **Princess scallops** — The smallest of the three. Delicate, petite, and less commonly seen at retail, but prized by chefs for canapés and elegant plating.

**Seasonality** is worth understanding. While scallops can technically be harvested year-round in UK waters, they spawn during late spring and summer. During this period, the meat thins as energy goes into reproduction. The widely accepted best months for British scallops are **November through March** — the colder months, when the flesh is plump, firm, and at its sweetest.

**The sustainability question** is one that matters deeply. The majority of UK scallops are caught using heavy-toothed dredgers — two-tonne steel frames dragged across the seabed. This method is efficient but devastating to marine habitats. The alternative is **diver-caught scallops** — hand-harvested by divers who select individual specimens without disturbing the surrounding ecosystem. They cost roughly twice as much, but the quality is often superior (no grit, no damage to the shell or flesh), and the environmental impact is incomparably smaller.

When you buy diver-caught, you're not just getting a better scallop. You're making a choice about the kind of sea you want to exist for future generations.

## Sourcing & Selecting the Best Ingredients

Great cooking begins long before the pan heats up. It begins at the counter — whether that's a fishmonger's slab, a supermarket display, or an online delivery box arriving at your door. Knowing what to look for transforms your results before you've lifted a knife.

### Choosing Your Squid

When selecting squid for chilli squid, look for:

- **Smell:** Fresh squid should smell clean and briny — like a rock pool on a clear day. Any strong, fishy, or ammonia-like odour means it's past its best.
- **Colour:** The body should be pale white to light pink, with a smooth, slightly glossy sheen. Avoid squid that looks dull, yellowed, or has dark spots.
- **Texture:** Firm but yielding. When you press the flesh gently, it should spring back. Mushy or slimy squid has been sitting too long.
- **Body vs tentacles:** For chilli squid, the body (tube) is what you want — it provides flat surfaces for scoring and even coating. Tentacles can be fried separately as a textural bonus but aren't essential.


**Fresh vs frozen:** Don't dismiss frozen squid. Much of the squid available in the UK has been frozen at sea immediately after catching, which locks in freshness. Thaw slowly overnight in the fridge, pat dry thoroughly, and it will perform beautifully.

### Choosing Your Scallops

The single most important decision here is **wet vs dry:**

- **Dry scallops** are untreated — shucked and kept cold, nothing more. They range in colour from ivory to pale coral or light orange. They smell sweet, like the sea. They sear perfectly because their surface isn't waterlogged.
- **Wet scallops** have been soaked in sodium tripolyphosphate (STPP), a chemical solution that causes them to absorb water. They appear unnaturally bright white and often sit in milky liquid. They're heavier (you're paying for water weight), they don't brown well, and they can have a faintly soapy aftertaste.

**Always ask your fishmonger:** "Are these dry scallops?" A good fishmonger will know immediately and respect you for asking. If buying from a supermarket, look for scallops labelled "dry" or "untreated," and avoid any sitting in visible liquid.

**Other things to look for:**
- Firm, springy flesh — not flabby or sticky
- A sweet, oceanic scent — never fishy
- Consistent size (for even cooking)
- The roe (orange crescent) attached or removed, depending on your preference — in the UK, the roe is often left on; it's edible and flavourful but can complicate searing, so for this recipe we remove it

### Where to Buy in England

- **Independent fishmongers** — Your best bet for quality and advice. They can tell you where the scallops were caught, whether they're diver-caught, and when they came in.
- **Supermarket fish counters** — Waitrose, M&S, and Sainsbury's often stock decent king scallops. Check for "dry" labelling.
- **Online delivery** — Specialists like The Fish Society, Direct Seafoods, and Fish for Thought deliver fresh and frozen seafood nationwide, often next-day.

### Storage

- Scallops keep in the fridge for up to two days but are best eaten on the day of purchase.
- Store them on a plate, covered with a damp cloth or cling film, on the coldest shelf.
- Squid should also be used within 1–2 days of purchase, stored in the fridge in its original packaging or a sealed container.

## Preparation & Technique

This is where the magic happens — and where most home cooks stumble. The good news? The mistakes are almost always the same ones, which means they're entirely avoidable once you understand what's happening and why.

Both chilli squid and scallops share one fundamental truth: **they cook fast, and they punish hesitation.** Respect their speed, and they'll reward you with textures that rival any restaurant plate in England.

### A. Prepping Chilli Squid for Maximum Crispiness

The goal is simple: a coating so crisp it shatters between your teeth, wrapped around squid so tender it barely resists the bite. Here's how to get there every time.

**Step 1: Clean and open the squid**

If your fishmonger hasn't already done this, pull the head and tentacles away from the body tube. Reach inside and remove the clear quill (a plastic-like backbone). Peel away any purple-grey skin from the outside of the tube. Rinse briefly under cold water and pat completely dry.

Slice the tube open along one side so it lies flat — you now have a rectangular sheet of squid.

**Step 2: Score the squid**

This step is not optional. Using a sharp knife, score the inside surface of the squid in a diagonal criss-cross diamond pattern, spacing the lines about 5mm apart. Don't cut all the way through — you want to go about halfway into the flesh.

Why does this matter? Three reasons:
1. It prevents the squid from curling into tight tubes when it hits hot oil
2. It creates ridges and pockets that hold the flour coating, giving you more crunch per bite
3. It increases the surface area, meaning more golden, crispy edges

After scoring, cut the squid into pieces roughly 3cm x 5cm.

**Step 3: Dry, dry, dry**

Pat every piece of squid with kitchen paper until it feels almost papery. Moisture is the enemy of crispness. Any water left on the surface will create steam in the fryer, turning your coating soggy rather than shattering.

**Step 4: The coating**

The secret that many UK home cooks miss: it's not about batter. It's about **dry starch coating.** The ideal blend is:

- **70% potato starch, 30% cornflour** — This creates the lightest, crispiest shell. Potato starch fries to a shatteringly delicate texture that cornflour alone can't achieve.
- If you can't find potato starch, **100% cornflour** works well — it just produces a slightly denser crunch.
- Season the flour mix with fine salt, white pepper, and chilli flakes before tossing the squid through it.


**Step 5: Frying**

Heat vegetable oil to **180C** in a deep pan or wok. Use enough oil that the squid pieces can float freely — roughly 5-7cm depth.

Fry in small batches. This is non-negotiable. Overcrowding the pan drops the oil temperature dramatically, which means the squid sits in lukewarm oil, absorbing fat and steaming rather than crisping. Three or four pieces at a time is plenty.

**Total frying time: 2 minutes.** That's it. Remove the squid the moment it turns pale golden. It will darken slightly as it drains. If you wait until it looks deeply golden in the oil, you've already overcooked it.

Drain on a wire rack (not kitchen paper, which can trap steam underneath and soften the base). Sprinkle with a little extra salt immediately.

**The science of rubbery squid:** Squid flesh is made of tightly coiled collagen fibres. Between 1-3 minutes of high heat, these fibres relax — the squid stays tender. Between 5-40 minutes, they contract violently, squeezing out moisture and turning the flesh tough and chewy. After 45+ minutes of gentle braising, they break down again into tenderness. For chilli squid, you're working in that short, beautiful first window. Don't miss it.

### B. Searing Scallops to Golden Perfection

If frying squid is about speed and crunch, searing scallops is about restraint and trust. You must trust the process — and resist the urge to fiddle.

**Step 1: Temper the scallops**

Remove scallops from the fridge 10 minutes before cooking. A fridge-cold scallop placed in a hot pan cools the surface, preventing the Maillard reaction (the chemical browning that creates that gorgeous golden crust). Room temperature on the outside, cold in the centre — that's the goal.

**Step 2: Dry them thoroughly**

This is the single most critical step. Take each scallop and press it firmly between two sheets of kitchen paper. You want the surface to feel almost tacky — not wet, not damp, but dry enough that your finger sticks slightly when you touch it. Any surface moisture will steam in the pan rather than sear, giving you a grey, pallid scallop instead of a golden one.

**Step 3: Season simply**

Just before cooking — not before — season one flat side of each scallop with flaky sea salt and a grind of black pepper. Seasoning too early draws out moisture (undoing all your drying work).

**Step 4: Get the pan screaming hot**

Use a heavy-based pan — cast iron is ideal. Heat it over high heat for 2-3 minutes until you see the faintest wisp of smoke rising from the surface. Add a thin film of oil with a high smoke point (vegetable, groundnut, or clarified butter).

**Step 5: Place and don't touch**

Lay each scallop seasoned-side down into the pan, placing them in a clockwise pattern (so you remember the order). Press gently for 2-3 seconds to ensure full contact with the hot surface. Then — and this is where courage comes in — **walk away.** Do not move them. Do not peek underneath. Do not nudge, press, or rearrange.


Listen. You should hear a confident, consistent sizzle — like applause. If it goes quiet, your pan wasn't hot enough.

**90 seconds.** That's your time on the first side.

**Step 6: Flip and baste**

Turn each scallop in the same clockwise order you placed them. The underside should be deeply golden — a rich amber crust that looks almost lacquered. If it's pale, give it another 20 seconds before flipping.

Now add a knob of butter, a lightly crushed garlic clove, and a sprig of thyme to the pan. As the butter foams and turns golden, tilt the pan and spoon that nutty, aromatic butter over the top of each scallop repeatedly for 30-60 seconds.

**Step 7: Know when to stop**

A perfectly cooked scallop has opaque, golden edges but retains a whisper of translucence in the very centre — like a setting sun seen through sea glass. The sides should feel gently springy, not firm.

Remove them immediately to a warm plate. Residual heat will carry them the final 10% of the way. If you wait until they look "fully cooked" in the pan, they'll be overcooked by the time they reach the table.

**The cardinal sins of scallop searing:**
- Wet surface (no crust forms)
- Cold pan (steaming instead of searing)
- Overcrowding (temperature drops, liquid pools)
- Moving them too soon (tears the developing crust)
- Cooking too long (rubber)

Avoid these five, and you'll produce scallops that belong on any fine-dining menu in England.

## The Chilli Element — Building Heat & Flavour

Heat is not just a sensation — it's a language. And in this dish, the chilli speaks to both the squid and the scallop in different ways. With the squid, it's a loud, joyful shout — integrated into the coating, tossed through at the last second, present in every bite. With the scallop, it's quieter — a drizzle, a dip, a delicate thread of fire that enhances without overwhelming.

Understanding how to build and control that heat is what separates a good plate from an extraordinary one.

### Types of Chilli to Use

Not all chillies are created equal, and each brings a different character:

- **Fresh red chillies** (medium heat) — The workhorse. Sliced into rings for garnish or deseeded and finely chopped for sauces. They bring fruity warmth without overwhelming the seafood.
- **Bird's eye chillies** (high heat) — For those who want genuine fire. Use sparingly — half a bird's eye, finely sliced, goes a long way. Beautiful in a dipping sauce where the heat hits you after the first taste of vinegar.
- **Dried chilli flakes** — Convenient and consistent. Mixed into the coating flour for chilli squid, they provide a background warmth that builds gradually with each piece.
- **Sichuan peppercorns** — Not technically a chilli but worth mentioning. They provide a unique numbing, tingling sensation (ma la) that pairs extraordinarily well with fried squid. Toast them in a dry pan until fragrant, crush, and add to your salt and pepper mix.


### Spice Blends Worth Knowing

- **Shichimi togarashi** — The seven-spice Japanese blend that Wagamama uses on their famous chilli squid. It contains chilli, sansho pepper, sesame seeds, nori, citrus peel, ginger, and poppy seeds. Sprinkle over freshly fried squid for that unmistakable restaurant flavour.
- **Classic salt and pepper mix** — Fine sea salt, ground white pepper, a pinch of five-spice powder, and chilli flakes. Simple. Effective. The backbone of every Chinese takeaway's salt and chilli squid.
- **Sichuan numbing salt** — Toasted and ground Sichuan peppercorns blended with flaky sea salt. Extraordinary sprinkled over scallops as they come out of the pan — the tingling sensation against the sweet scallop meat is addictive.

### Balancing Heat with Acidity

Fire without relief becomes exhausting. The key to making chilli work in seafood dishes is always giving the palate somewhere to rest — and that rest comes from acidity:

- **Lime juice** — A squeeze of fresh lime over both the squid and scallops just before serving. The citrus cuts through the richness of frying oil and butter, brightening everything it touches.
- **Malt vinegar** — The base of the classic spicy vinegar dip. Deeply British, slightly sweet, and the perfect foil to chilli heat. Mixed with soy sauce and sugar, it creates that addictive Wagamama-style sauce.
- **Rice vinegar** — Lighter and more delicate than malt vinegar. Use in Asian-style dressings or as a subtle splash in the dipping sauce for a softer acidity.
- **Sweet chilli sauce** — The safety net. For those who want warmth without intensity, a drizzle of sweet chilli alongside offers heat with enough sugar to cushion the blow.

### Making a Chilli Paste from Scratch

For a more complex, rounded heat — particularly beautiful spooned over seared scallops — consider making a quick chilli paste:

Blend together:
- 3 fresh red chillies (deseeded if you prefer milder heat)
- A thumb of fresh ginger, peeled
- 2 shallots, roughly chopped
- 2 garlic cloves
- 1 tbsp fish sauce
- A pinch of salt
- 2 tbsp water

Blitz until smooth. This paste can be stirred through a hot wok with the squid in the final seconds of cooking, or thinned with lime juice and drizzled over scallops on the plate. It keeps in the fridge for up to a week and improves as the flavours meld.

## Bringing It Together — Serving Chilli Squid & Scallops

You've sourced beautifully. You've prepped with care. You've built your heat profile. Now comes the moment that transforms ingredients into an experience — the way you bring chilli squid and scallops together on a plate, and what you choose to surround them with.

This is where cooking becomes storytelling.

### Plating: Starter, Sharing Plate, or Main Event?

The beauty of this pairing is its versatility. It shifts effortlessly depending on the occasion:

**As a starter:** Serve two or three seared scallops nestled beside a small tumble of crispy chilli squid. Keep the plate clean — a swoosh of pea puree, a scattering of fresh red chilli rings, a wedge of charred lime. This is restraint. This is anticipation. You're setting the tone for what's to come.

**As a sharing plate:** Pile the chilli squid high on a wooden board or a warm platter. Arrange the scallops around the edges like golden jewels. Place dipping sauces in small bowls — spicy vinegar on one side, garlic mayo on the other. Scatter fresh herbs, toasted peanuts, and sliced spring onions across the top. This is abundance. This is a table where people reach across each other, where fingers get sticky, where conversation flows because the food has given everyone permission to relax.

**As a main course:** Build a bed of Asian-style noodles or a crisp salad of shredded mooli, cucumber ribbons, and fresh mint. Lay the seared scallops on top, lean the chilli squid against them, and drizzle a chilli-lime dressing over everything. Add substance with jasmine rice or warm flatbread on the side. This is a complete meal — satisfying, balanced, and impressive enough for any dinner party.

### Side Dishes That Sing Alongside

Both chilli squid and scallops are bold enough to stand alone, but the right accompaniments elevate the plate from excellent to unforgettable:

- **Pea puree** — The classic British pairing for scallops. Its gentle sweetness and vibrant green colour create a natural canvas. Blend fresh or frozen peas with a knob of butter, a splash of stock, and a pinch of mint.

- **Black pudding crumbs** — Crumble and pan-fry until crisp. The earthy, savoury depth of black pudding against sweet scallops is a combination that has graced British fine dining menus for decades. It works because it shouldn't — and yet it does, magnificently.

- **Charred lime halves** — Place lime halves cut-side down in a hot, dry pan until blackened. The caramelisation deepens the citrus flavour and adds a smoky sweetness when squeezed over the squid.


- **Asian slaw** — Finely shredded red cabbage, carrot, and spring onion dressed with rice vinegar, sesame oil, and a whisper of honey. It provides crunch and freshness to cut through the richness of the fried squid.

- **Crushed peanuts and fresh mint** — A Southeast Asian-inspired garnish that adds texture, fragrance, and a nutty warmth. Scatter generously over the top just before serving.

- **Cauliflower puree** — Silky smooth, subtly sweet, and neutral enough to let both the squid and scallops shine. Cook cauliflower florets in milk until tender, then blend with butter and white pepper until velvety.

- **Warm sourdough** — Sometimes simplicity wins. Thick slices of toasted sourdough rubbed with garlic and drizzled with good olive oil give people something to mop up every last drop of sauce with.

### Drink Pairings

What you pour alongside matters more than many people realise. The heat from the chilli, the sweetness of the scallop, and the richness of the fried squid all need something that refreshes rather than competes:

- **White wine:** A crisp, dry Albarino or a Picpoul de Pinet — both have the minerality and acidity to stand up to chilli heat while complementing seafood's natural salinity. For something more aromatic, try a dry Riesling from Alsace — its faint sweetness tames the fire without overwhelming the delicate scallop.

- **Sparkling:** English sparkling wine (yes, really) — its fine bubbles and bright acidity cleanse the palate between bites of crispy squid. Or Champagne, if the occasion calls for it.

- **Beer:** A cold Japanese lager like Asahi or Kirin — clean, light, and designed for exactly this kind of food. Alternatively, a wheat beer with its subtle citrus notes works beautifully.

- **Non-alcoholic:** Sparkling water with a generous squeeze of lime and a few slices of fresh ginger. Or a chilled lemongrass and ginger iced tea — fragrant, refreshing, and unexpectedly perfect.

### Taking It Outside: The Barbecue Approach

There's something about open flame and seafood that speaks to our most ancient instincts. And both chilli squid and scallops take to a barbecue with extraordinary grace.

Chef Nathan Outlaw — one of England's finest seafood cooks — champions this approach: chilli squid charred quickly over fierce heat and served with a smoky aubergine dip, alongside scallops grilled in their half-shells with a sweetcorn chutney spooned over the top.

The key to barbecuing these:

- **Squid:** Brush with oil and the chilli marinade. Lay flat on the hottest part of the grill. One minute per side — no more. The char adds a smokiness that no kitchen hob can replicate.

- **Scallops:** Place directly on a clean, oiled grate or cook them in their half-shells. Two to three minutes with the lid down creates gentle, smoky heat. Finish with a squeeze of lime and a flick of chilli oil.

The result is primal, celebratory, and impossibly good — especially on a warm English summer evening with a cold glass in hand and the smell of charcoal in the air.

## Recipe: Chilli Squid & Seared Scallops with Spicy Vinegar Dip

*Everything we've talked about, distilled into one beautiful plate.*

---

**Serves:** 2 as a main / 4 as a starter
**Prep time:** 20 minutes
**Cook time:** 10 minutes
**Difficulty:** Medium

---

### Ingredients

**For the Chilli Squid:**
- 300g squid tubes (cleaned, quills removed)
- 35g potato starch (or cornflour)
- 15g cornflour (if using potato starch above — for the 70/30 blend)
- 1 tsp fine sea salt
- 1/2 tsp white pepper
- 1/2 tsp chilli flakes (or 1 tsp shichimi powder for a Wagamama-style finish)
- Vegetable oil, for deep frying (about 500ml)

**For the Seared Scallops:**
- 6 large king scallops (dry scallops preferred, roe removed)
- 1 tbsp vegetable oil or clarified butter
- 15g unsalted butter
- 1 small garlic clove, lightly crushed
- 1 sprig fresh thyme (optional)
- Flaky sea salt and black pepper

**For the Spicy Vinegar Dip:**
- 50g caster sugar
- 2 tbsp water
- 5 tbsp malt vinegar
- 5 tbsp light soy sauce
- Pinch of sea salt
- 1 red chilli, deseeded and finely chopped
- 3 sprigs fresh coriander, finely chopped

**To Garnish:**
- 2 spring onions, finely sliced on the diagonal
- 1 fresh red chilli, sliced into rings
- 1 lime, cut into wedges
- Fresh coriander leaves

---

### Method

#### Step 1: Make the Spicy Vinegar Dip (can be done ahead)

1. Combine the caster sugar and water in a small saucepan over medium heat. Stir until the sugar dissolves completely — do not let it boil.
2. Remove from heat. Stir in the malt vinegar, light soy sauce, and sea salt.
3. Allow to cool to room temperature, then stir through the chopped red chilli and fresh coriander.
4. Transfer to a small serving bowl and set aside. This keeps well in the fridge for up to 3 days.


#### Step 2: Prepare the Squid

1. Lay the squid tubes flat on a chopping board. Using a sharp knife, score the inside surface in a diagonal criss-cross diamond pattern — be careful not to cut all the way through. This helps the squid curl beautifully when it hits the hot oil and creates extra surface area for the coating to grip.
2. Slice the scored squid into pieces roughly 3cm x 5cm.
3. Pat the squid pieces thoroughly dry with kitchen paper. This is essential — any moisture will prevent crispiness.

#### Step 3: Coat and Fry the Squid

1. In a shallow bowl, combine the potato starch, cornflour, salt, white pepper, and chilli flakes. Mix well.
2. Heat the vegetable oil in a deep pan or wok to 180C. If you don't have a thermometer, drop a pinch of flour into the oil — it should sizzle immediately and float to the surface.
3. Toss the squid pieces in the flour mixture, ensuring every piece is evenly and thoroughly coated. Shake off any excess.
4. Fry in small batches (do not overcrowd the pan) for **2 minutes only** — until pale golden and crisp. The squid will continue to firm slightly as it rests.
5. Remove with a slotted spoon and drain on a wire rack set over kitchen paper. Sprinkle immediately with a little extra sea salt.
6. Keep warm in a low oven (120C) while you sear the scallops.

#### Step 4: Sear the Scallops

1. Remove scallops from the fridge 10 minutes before cooking. Pat each one dry with kitchen paper — press firmly to remove as much surface moisture as possible.
2. Season one flat side of each scallop with a pinch of flaky sea salt and a grind of black pepper.
3. Heat a heavy-based frying pan (cast iron is ideal) over high heat until it just begins to smoke. Add the vegetable oil.
4. Place scallops seasoned-side down into the pan, one at a time, in a clockwise pattern. **Do not move them.** Press gently for the first few seconds to ensure full contact with the pan.
5. Cook for **90 seconds** without touching. You should hear a consistent, confident sizzle — if it goes quiet, your pan isn't hot enough.
6. Flip each scallop in the same order you placed them. Add the butter, crushed garlic, and thyme sprig to the pan. As the butter foams, tilt the pan and baste the scallops with the golden butter for 30-60 seconds.
7. The scallops are done when the edges are opaque and golden, but the very centre still holds a whisper of translucence. Remove immediately to a warm plate.

#### Step 5: Plate and Serve

1. Arrange the seared scallops on a warmed plate or platter — three per person if serving as a starter.
2. Pile the crispy chilli squid alongside or in a small mound next to the scallops.
3. Scatter sliced spring onions, fresh chilli rings, and coriander leaves over the top.
4. Tuck lime wedges to the side.
5. Serve the spicy vinegar dip in a small bowl alongside.

**Serve immediately.** Both elements are at their best within minutes of cooking.


---

### Cook's Notes

- **Timing is everything.** Make the dip first, prep the squid, then fry the squid and sear the scallops back-to-back. The whole cooking process takes under 10 minutes once your oil is hot.
- **Don't skip the scoring.** The diamond pattern on the squid isn't decorative — it prevents curling into tubes and creates pockets that hold the coating.
- **Batch frying is non-negotiable.** Overcrowding drops the oil temperature, resulting in soggy, greasy squid rather than crisp, light pieces.
- **Residual heat matters.** Pull scallops from the pan when they look 90% done. The final 10% happens on the plate as they rest for 30 seconds.
- **Substitution:** If you can't find potato starch, use 50g cornflour on its own. The result will be slightly less shatteringly crisp but still delicious.
- **Scaling up:** This recipe doubles easily for 4 people. Sear scallops in two batches rather than crowding the pan.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### 1. How do I stop my squid from being chewy and rubbery?

The single biggest mistake is overcooking. Squid has a narrow window of perfection: it's either cooked very quickly (under 2-3 minutes over high heat) or braised very slowly (45 minutes or more over low heat). Anything in between — that 5-to-40-minute danger zone — and the proteins seize up, turning the flesh tough and gummy.

For chilli squid, your total frying time should be **2 minutes maximum** in oil at 180C. Remove the pieces the moment they turn pale gold. They'll firm slightly as they rest. If they're browning deeply in the fryer, you've already gone too far.

Also ensure your squid is thoroughly dry before coating. Moisture creates steam, which fights against crispness and can extend cooking time — both enemies of tender squid.

### 2. Can scallops be served rare?

Yes, absolutely. In fact, many chefs — including Gordon Ramsay and those across fine-dining kitchens in Britain — prefer scallops with a seared, caramelised exterior and a centre that remains slightly translucent and cool. This is perfectly safe with fresh, high-quality scallops.

BBC Good Food confirms that scallops are regularly served rare. The key is sourcing: buy the freshest scallops you can find (ideally day-caught), keep them refrigerated, and cook them the same day. If they smell briny and sweet like the sea rather than fishy, they're perfect for rare preparation.


### 3. Are scallops halal?

This is a question with no single definitive answer, as Islamic scholars hold differing views on shellfish. The mainstream position — particularly within the Hanafi school — leans toward scallops being halal, as they are a type of sea creature and the Quran broadly permits food from the sea. However, some scholars within certain traditions classify only fish with scales as halal.

The consensus advice from Islamic food authorities is that Muslims should research this question within their own school of thought and come to an informed personal conclusion. If in doubt, consult a trusted local scholar.

### 4. If I'm allergic to shellfish, can I eat squid or scallops?

Both squid and scallops are classified as molluscs, which fall within the broader "shellfish" category. Shellfish allergies typically react to a protein called tropomyosin, which is present in both crustaceans (prawns, crab, lobster) and molluscs (squid, scallops, mussels, oysters).

However, allergies vary between individuals. Some people react only to crustaceans and tolerate molluscs without issue, while others react to both. **You should always consult your GP or allergist before consuming either squid or scallops if you have a known shellfish allergy.** Carry antihistamines or an EpiPen if advised, and never assume safety without professional guidance.

### 5. How many calories are in a typical serving of chilli squid?

A standard restaurant portion of chilli squid (such as Wagamama's famous version) contains approximately **400-500 kcal**, depending on portion size, coating thickness, and accompaniments. The squid itself is lean and high in protein — most of the calories come from the deep-frying oil absorbed by the batter.

For a lighter version at home, you can reduce the calorie count significantly by:
- Using a very thin coating of cornflour (rather than a thick batter)
- Frying at the correct temperature (180C — hotter oil means less absorption)
- Draining thoroughly on a wire rack
- Serving smaller portions alongside seared scallops (which are naturally low in fat at roughly 100 kcal per 3 large scallops)

### 6. What's the difference between wet and dry scallops — and does it really matter?

It matters enormously. "Wet" scallops have been soaked in a solution of sodium tripolyphosphate (STPP) after harvesting. This chemical causes them to absorb water, which plumps them up (making them appear larger and heavier — meaning you pay more for water weight) and extends shelf life. The downsides: they release that excess liquid when heated, which pools in the pan, prevents browning, and can leave a faintly chemical, soapy aftertaste.

"Dry" scallops are untreated — they're simply shucked and kept cold. They have a naturally sweet, concentrated flavour, a firmer texture, and they sear beautifully because their surface isn't waterlogged.

**How to tell the difference:** Dry scallops range from ivory to slightly pink or orange in colour. Wet scallops are unnaturally bright white and often sit in a pool of milky liquid in the packet. When in doubt, ask your fishmonger directly — a good one will always know.


### 7. When is scallop season in the UK, and when are they at their best?

Technically, there is no strict closed season for scallops in UK waters — they can be harvested year-round. However, scallops spawn during late spring and summer (roughly May to August), during which time their meat can be thinner and less sweet as they expend energy on reproduction.

The widely accepted **best months for British scallops are November through to March** — the colder months, when the meat is plump, firm, and at its sweetest. This is when you'll find them on the menus of Britain's best restaurants, and when fishmongers have the finest specimens available.

If you're buying diver-caught scallops from specific regions (Lyme Bay, Shetland, Scottish West Coast), your fishmonger can often tell you the exact harvest date — a luxury that speaks to the freshness and traceability of British seafood at its best.
